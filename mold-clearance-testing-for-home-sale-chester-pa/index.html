Mold Clearance Testing Before a Home Sale in Chester, PA

Clearance testing checks that a cleanup worked. Here is when it makes sense and what the guidance says.

☎ (856) 702-0892

By the Chester Water Damage Pros team

Mold comes up in home sales more often than people expect. A buyer sees a stain, an inspector flags a smell or a seller remembers a leak from years ago. Clearance testing is one way to document that remediation was done, but it is not required in every case.

This guide explains what clearance testing is, what the EPA says about sampling and how to decide whether it fits your situation.

What clearance testing is

Clearance testing happens after remediation. A qualified person inspects the work and may collect air or surface samples to compare the cleaned area with an outdoor or unaffected reference. The aim is to confirm the area is clean, dry and ready for rebuild.

It differs from testing done to look for mold. Testing for presence is rarely needed when growth is visible.

What the EPA says about sampling

EPA guidance says that if visible mold growth is present, sampling is unnecessary. It also says no federal limits have been set for mold or mold spores, so test results have no pass-fail line from the federal government. Sampling can help in specific cases, such as confirming a cleanup.

That means a test report needs interpretation by someone qualified, and the report is not a certificate. Ask what standard or reference the tester compares against.

When clearance testing makes sense

  • A buyer, lender or agent asks for documentation of a past cleanup.
  • The remediation was large and you want an independent check.
  • There was a dispute about whether the work was complete.
  • You want a dated record for your files.

It makes less sense when the only goal is reassurance after a very small cleanup. A visual check that the area is dry and clean may be enough.

Independence matters

If testing is meant to check the work, the person testing should be independent of the company that did the cleanup. Ask who does the testing, how samples are taken and what the report will say.

Ask whether the remediation followed ANSI/IICRC S520 and whether the contractor will provide a written scope and a record of moisture readings. The mold remediation page describes what that record includes.

Documents that help at sale time

  1. The original cause of the water and the repair of the source.
  2. The remediation scope and dates.
  3. Moisture readings showing the area is dry.
  4. Photos before, during and after.
  5. Any clearance report with the tester's name and method.

Disclosure rules are a matter for your real estate professional or attorney. Keep the records organized so they can advise you.

Fix the water first

A clean test means little if the source still leaks. Confirm the cause is fixed and that humidity is under control before you test or list the home. The basement water damage page explains common sources in below-grade spaces.

EPA says the key to mold control is moisture control, and that applies before and after a sale.

What a clearance report should include

  • The tester's name, qualifications and independence from the remediation contractor.
  • The date, the areas tested and the sampling method.
  • The reference used for comparison, such as an outdoor sample.
  • Observations about moisture and visible conditions.
  • Plain-language findings and limits of the testing.

A report that is only a table of numbers with no explanation is hard for a buyer to use. Ask the tester to explain it in writing.

Buyers and sellers

If you are selling, collect your records before listing. A folder with the repair history answers questions before they become negotiating points. If you are buying, ask the seller for any remediation records and for the cause and fix of the water event.

A buyer who finds staining can ask for a moisture reading and for the scope of past work. Both sides are better served by documents than by assurances.

Limits of any test

A sample is a snapshot. It reflects the day, the spot and the method used, and it can change with weather, activity in the home and how long doors and windows have been closed. Two tests a week apart can read differently without anything having changed in the building.

That is why the report should be read alongside the visual inspection and the moisture readings. A tester who reads only the lab number and ignores a wet wall is not giving you the full picture.

If the report raises questions, ask the tester to explain what they mean and what, if anything, to do next.

A short decision guide

  1. Was there visible growth that was removed by a professional? If so, ask for the scope and moisture records.
  2. Does a buyer, lender or agent specifically ask for testing? If so, hire an independent tester.
  3. Is the source fixed and the area dry? If not, address that before anything else.
  4. Are you unsure what applies to the sale? Ask your real estate professional or attorney.

Following these four questions in order avoids paying for a test that nobody asked for, and avoids skipping one that somebody will.

Chester, PA: ask the city what applies to a sale

Home sales in Chester proceed within the city's rules for inspections. The City of Chester Licenses and Inspections office posts forms and information on its website, and it is the right place to ask what applies to a sale or a repair.

Buyers in Aston and Brookhaven should ask their own municipality, since requirements differ by township and borough.

Clearance testing questions from Chester homeowners

Do I need clearance testing to sell?

Not necessarily. Ask your real estate professional or attorney.

What does a clearance test cost?

Prices vary by the tester and the home, and we do not quote numbers here.

Can the remediation company test its own work?

It can, but independent testing gives a clearer check.

Does a pass mean mold will never return?

No. Moisture decides that. Keep the area dry.
