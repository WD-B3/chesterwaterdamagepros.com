Water Damage Assessment: What to Expect in Chester, PA

Moisture meters, thermal cameras and a written scope. What a real assessment includes.

☎ (856) 702-0892

By the Chester Water Damage Pros team

A water damage assessment answers four questions: where did the water come from, what kind of water is it, what is wet and what can be saved. A good one is a physical inspection with instruments, not a quick look and a number.

If you have booked an assessment or are comparing companies, this guide explains what the visit involves and what you should receive when it is done.

What the inspector looks for first

The first question is the source. A stopped source means the damage is no longer growing. If the source is still active, the inspector will tell you to shut it off before anything else happens.

Next comes the water category. Clean supply water, gray water from appliances and contaminated water from sewage or flooding need different protection and disposal. The ANSI/IICRC S500 standard describes these categories, and you can ask the company whether its crews follow it.

The tools of an assessment

ToolWhat it shows
Moisture meterA numeric reading for drywall, wood and subfloor.
Thermal imaging cameraCool, wet areas behind paint and under flooring.
HygrometerAir temperature and humidity, which affect drying.
Visual inspectionStaining, swelling, odor, rust and mineral crust.

A thermal camera does not read moisture directly. It shows temperature differences that suggest wet areas, and the meter confirms them.

The moisture map and the dry standard

Readings are taken across the affected area and in an unaffected area of the same house for comparison. That unaffected reading is the dry standard for your building. Every house has a different baseline, so a number alone does not mean much until it is compared.

The inspector marks wet areas on a plan or in photos. That map decides where equipment goes and which materials may need removal.

What you should receive afterward

  • A written scope listing rooms, materials and recommended work.
  • Readings and photos that show what was found.
  • An explanation of what can be dried in place and what likely must be removed.
  • A plain statement of what is not yet known, such as hidden cavities.
  • Scope and price explained before work starts.

Be wary of a verbal diagnosis with no readings. If a company will not show you numbers, ask why.

Questions to ask

Ask what category of water the inspector believes it is and why. Ask how drying will be monitored and how the company will decide when equipment can leave. Ask whether the work will follow ANSI/IICRC S500, and what happens if readings do not drop.

The drying process is explained on the structural drying page, and damage inside finished walls is covered on the wall and drywall water damage page.

A walk-through of the visit

Most visits follow the same order. The inspector talks with you about what happened and when, then looks at the source and the areas you point out. Meters and a thermal camera come out next, and readings are taken room by room. Photos and notes are taken as the inspector goes.

You can follow along. Ask to see the meter and the camera screen, and ask what the numbers mean. A good inspector explains why a wall that looks dry is still wet inside, and why a wet-looking carpet may be fine to dry in place.

Hidden moisture and why it matters

Water does not stay where it lands. It moves down through floors, along studs and into insulation. A ceiling stain may sit above a wet wall, and a wet carpet may sit above a damp slab or subfloor. An assessment that stops at the visible damage can miss the part that causes trouble later.

That is why readings matter more than looks. Materials can feel dry and still hold enough moisture to swell, stain or support mold. Drying targets are set from your own dry standard, and equipment stays until readings reach it.

How to prepare for the visit

  • Shut off the source and note when you did.
  • Move valuables, papers and electronics to a dry spot.
  • Write down what you saw, when you saw it and what you have done so far.
  • Have your insurance information handy in case you decide to file.
  • Clear a path to the basement, crawl space or attic if those areas are involved.

A short written timeline helps the inspector place the damage. It also helps your insurer, who will ask the same questions.

Assessment questions from Chester homeowners

How long does an assessment take?

It depends on the size of the loss. A single room takes less time than a whole floor, and a careful moisture map takes longer than a quick look.

Will the assessment cost money?

Companies handle this differently, so ask before the visit. Ask for scope and price to be explained before any work starts.

Can the company see inside walls?

A thermal camera and a meter read through paint, and small drilled holes allow a probe or a borescope. The inspector may recommend opening a small section.

What if I disagree with the scope?

Ask for the readings behind each recommendation. A second opinion is reasonable for a large loss.
