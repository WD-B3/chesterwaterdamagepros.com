How to use the town pages

Each town page is written for that place. It lists the ZIP codes covered, describes the kinds of houses and water problems common there, and explains what to do first when water is spreading. Read the page for your town if you have time, and call first if you do not.

What happens when you call from a nearby town

The dispatcher asks for your address first, because it decides which crews can respond. Then comes the same short set of questions for every call: where the water is coming from, how deep it is, whether it is near electrical equipment and how long it has been there.

Same-day response is offered when a crew is available. The dispatcher tells you plainly what to expect, and explains the first steps you can take while you wait.

Flood maps and insurance across the area

Flood risk varies by address, not by town. Check any address you are concerned about on the FEMA Flood Map Service Center, and ask your insurer whether flood damage is covered, because standard homeowners policies often exclude it. The Pennsylvania Insurance Department advises homeowners to check coverage with their insurer each year.

Why local knowledge matters in water damage

Water follows the building, not the map. A 1920s twin in one borough and a 1960s split-level in a township a few miles away fail in different ways. Older houses tend to have plaster, long pipe runs and block basements, while newer construction leans on drywall, plywood and engineered flooring. Each dries differently.

That is why the dispatcher asks what kind of house it is, roughly how old it is and what the water has touched. The answers decide which equipment goes in and which materials get checked first.

The same applies to commercial space. A shop in a shared building has neighbors and shared walls, so the first questions include whether water is moving to another unit.
